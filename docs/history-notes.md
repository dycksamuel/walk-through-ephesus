## How to read this file

Every claim is sorted into one of three bins:

- **K** = known from archaeology or ancient texts
- **R** = reasonable reconstruction (evidence points this way, but it is an inference)
- **I** = invented for the game

How each item was checked:

- **[web]** = confirmed by a web search during this review (source named).
- **[memory]** = from my own knowledge of the literature, not re-checked online. Treat as "probably right, confirm before it goes on a plaque."
- **[unverified]** = I could not confirm it either way.

Network note: web *search* worked, but opening pages directly was blocked for `www.oeaw.ac.at`, `epigraphy.packhum.org` and `en.wikipedia.org` (not in the session's allowed domains). So I could read search summaries but not the Austrian excavation pages or the inscription database themselves. Where a date rests on a search summary only, I say so.

## A. Required changes, most important first

### A1. The council house (location 14) did not exist on that spot in the 50s
- **Problem.** The plan puts a "simpler hall marked reconstruction" where the 2nd-century council house stands, under the heading "known from archaeology." The full publication of the building (Bier, *The Bouleuterion at Ephesos*, Forschungen in Ephesos IX/5, 2011) dates its *first* phase to about AD 100 and found no evidence of an earlier council house underneath. An earlier one was proposed by Alzinger but is not proven. [web, search summary of Bier and of Balty's review]
- **Fix.** Move the council house to the "imagined placements" table. Plaque: "Ephesus certainly had a city council in the 50s (K), but where it met is unknown. The council house you can visit today was built about fifty years later. This hall is imagined (I)."
- **Bin.** Council existing: K. Building here in the 50s: I.

### A2. The theatre: do not say "being enlarged, with scaffolding" as fact
- **Problem.** Sources disagree. Older handbooks say the Roman rebuilding began under Claudius and that the stage building got two storeys under Nero. The current Austrian Academy publications (Styhler-Aydın on the auditorium; Öztürk and others on the stage) put the first theatre in the 2nd century BC, and the big transformation in the Flavian period, with a stage-front inscription dated AD 85 and the auditorium enlarged in stages into the 2nd century. [web, search summaries; I could not open the Academy pages]
- **What is safe for AD 53–55.** A Hellenistic theatre on the west slope of Panayırdağ, already large, with a simpler stage building and without the top tier seen today (K for existence, R for its exact form). The well-known figure of 21,500–25,000 seats is for the finished Roman theatre and must not be used for the 50s. No seat count for the 50s is known.
- **Fix.** Plaque: "The theatre Paul's friends were dragged into was the older Greek theatre, smaller than the ruin you can visit; most of what stands today was built in the fifty years after the riot." Drop the scaffolding or label it imagined. Fragment 5 ("top rows") can stay; just don't model the third tier.
- **Bin.** Theatre exists, riot there: K. Size and look in the 50s: R. Scaffolding: I.

### A3. "Free city" is probably wrong (soldier's dialogue, section 4)
- **Problem.** Ephesus was the seat of the Roman proconsul and an assize centre. One academic study of Acts 19 states it was a tribute-paying city, not a *civitas libera*, in Paul's day; popular sites say the opposite. [web, conflicting; unresolved]
- **Fix.** Replace "keeping the peace in a free city" with "a Greek city that runs its own affairs under the eye of the Roman governor." That is exactly the tension in the town clerk's speech (Acts 19:38–40) and needs no legal label.
- **Bin.** Self-governing city under a proconsul: K. "Free city" status: unverified.

### A4. The soldier: plausible, but not as a policeman, and not in parade armour
- **Is he plausible?** Yes, with care. Asia had no legion and no regular garrison [web, secondary]. But governors had soldiers on their staff, and small detachments are attested in Asian cities; soldiers' inscriptions exist at Ephesus, mostly undated or later [web, secondary; memory]. So: one soldier seconded to the proconsul's staff, as escort, messenger or guard at an official building. **R.**
- **What he was not.** City order was the job of city officials. In Acts 19 nobody calls soldiers; the town clerk ends the riot. Keep the soldier out of the riot scene entirely.
- **Kit for the 50s** [memory; standard reference: Bishop and Coulston, *Roman Military Equipment*]:
  - Day to day on staff duty: tunic, cloak, hobnailed sandal-boots (caligae), military belt with dagger and sword. The belt and sword were what marked a man as a soldier. Probably no armour or shield while standing about in town.
  - Full kit, which he can describe or have on a stand: bronze or iron helmet with cheek pieces and neck guard; body armour, most likely a mail shirt (segmented plate armour existed by now but was not universal, and was mainly worn by legionaries in the West); short sword of the "Mainz" type; dagger; large shield, either the legionary's curved shield or an auxiliary's flat oval one; javelin or spear.
  - Avoid: the all-identical red-and-plate "movie legionary," leather armour, stirrups, wrist bracers, a centurion's crest on a common soldier.
- **Link to Eph 6.** Paul's word for shield (*thyreos*) is the big door-shaped shield, so a large shield is right. Two honesty points for the journal: the armour list also echoes Isaiah 59:17, 11:5 and 52:7 (and Wisdom 5:17–20), so it is not simply "Paul looking at a soldier"; and the list has no spear.
- **Bin.** A soldier in Ephesus: R. This soldier: I.

### A5. Riot outline (section 6) departs from Acts 19 in four places
1. **The chant starts earlier.** Acts 19:28 has the craftsmen shout "Great is Artemis of the Ephesians!" straight after Demetrius's speech, *before* the rush to the theatre. Step 4 says the chant "begins" with Alexander. Fix: first chant at step 1; at step 4 it *resumes* and lasts two hours.
2. **Why the crowd shouts Alexander down.** Acts 19:34: "when they perceived that he was a Jew." The plan leaves this out. It is in the text and it matters for Eph 2:11–18, so state it plainly and without relish. Acts does not say what Alexander meant to say; do not invent it.
3. **Nobody among Gaius, Aristarchus and Alexander speaks in Acts.** Section 4 says "their words come from Acts 19." They have no words. Gaius and Aristarchus are "seized" (19:29), which is stronger than "swept along." Alexander only motions with his hand.
4. **Demetrius's speech was not in the theatre.** He gathers the craftsmen first (19:25), and the theatre comes after. Stage step 1 at the workshops, or show it as a short "earlier that day" cut. Also add to the narration that some Asiarchs, "being his friends," sent Paul a message not to go in (19:31), alongside the disciples (19:30).

Everything else in the outline matches Acts 19:23–41 and 20:1. **K** (as text).

### A6. The framing note on Ephesians understates the authorship debate
- **Problem.** "Most date it to AD 60–62 … some scholars think a student of Paul wrote it later" tilts the scales. Raymond Brown estimated about 80% of critical scholars judge Paul did not write it; Harold Hoehner's count of commentaries found 54% for Paul, 39% against, 7% unsure. Who is "most" depends on who is counted. [web, via secondary summary]
- **Fix (suggested wording).** "The letter says it is from Paul, in prison. The traditional view, held by many scholars, is that Paul wrote it about AD 60–62, probably from Rome. Many other scholars think a follower wrote it in Paul's name a decade or more after his death. The oldest copies do not have the words 'in Ephesus' in the first verse, so it may have been meant for several churches in the region. Either way, it was written after the events in this game."
- The "at Ephesus" point is sound: the words are missing from Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus [web] and from Papyrus 46 [memory].
- **Bin.** K (state of scholarship).

### A7. Temple of Artemis: three corrections
- **"127 columns."** Only Pliny (*Natural History* 36.95) says so; modern reconstructions of the plan do not all reach that number. Keep the plan's "(Pliny)" and word the plaque "Pliny counted 127." **K** that Pliny says it; the true number is uncertain.
- **"About four times the footprint of the Parthenon."** This is true only using Pliny's own figures (425 × 225 Roman feet, about 126 × 67 m, about 8,400 m², against the Parthenon's roughly 31 × 70 m, about 2,150 m²). Excavated figures quoted for the temple platform are smaller (about 105–115 m × 51–55 m in the sources I could find, which do not agree with each other), giving roughly two and a half to three times. [web for the range; exact metric dimensions unverified]. Fix: "about three times the floor area of the Parthenon, and by Pliny's measurements nearly four."
- **It faced west.** Unusually for a Greek temple, the entrance and the altar court were on the west side. [memory; an early excavation report found by search says the earlier temples were entered from the west "like its successors"]. Build the front and altar facing west, toward the sea, which is also the side the player arrives from.
- **Sightlines.** The temple lay in the plain north-east of Panayırdağ. The hill hides it from the theatre (which faces west) and from the state agora. It could be seen from the north and east slopes and along the Sacred Road. If the game shows it from elsewhere, say that is a liberty.
- **Skyline.** Ayasuluk hill behind the temple must be bare of the Basilica of St John, the castle and the İsa Bey mosque.
- Also right for the 50s: this is the temple rebuilt after the fire of 356 BC, and it stood until the Gothic raid of AD 262 [memory].

### A8. Octagon: do not call it Arsinoe's tomb
- The Octagon is a late 1st-century BC tomb monument, so it is correctly included. But the famous identification with Cleopatra's sister Arsinoe IV was always a hypothesis, and a January 2025 study (Weber and others, *Scientific Reports* 15, 943) showed the skull from the tomb belonged to a boy of about 11–14. [web]
- **Fix.** Plaque: "An eight-sided tomb, about 80 years old in Paul's day. Who was buried here is not known."
- Also call these "tombs and monuments," not "honorary monuments": the Octagon and the Heroon are tombs or hero shrines; only the Memmius Monument is honorary.

### A9. Hill names: usable, but they are a scholarly convention
- Pion = Panayırdağ and Preon = Bülbüldağ follows the current Austrian usage, based on Engelmann (*ZPE* 89, 1991), who identifies Preon with Bülbüldağ. [web] It is defensible.
- But it is not settled. Older books and most guidebooks call Bülbüldağ "Mount Koressos"; some older scholarship treats Pion and Preon as two spellings of one name; and a later Engelmann article is reported as applying "Koressos" to the Panayırdağ side. Koressos is now usually taken as a district or harbour settlement on the north side, which fits the "Koressian Gate" near the stadium. [web, conflicting]
- **Fix.** Put the modern Turkish names first on the map with the ancient name in brackets ("Panayırdağ (probably ancient Pion)"), and add one plaque line: "Scholars still argue about which ancient name belongs to which hill." Do not use "Koressos" for a hill.

### C2. The "Neronian hall" on the agora's east side
The dedication (search summary of the inscription database) is to Artemis, the deified Claudius, Nero, Agrippina and the people, dated AD 54–59. [web] Claudius died in October 54, so the hall was dedicated after that and before Agrippina's death in 59. During Paul's stay it was at most a building site.
- **Fix.** Either leave it out, or show the east side of the agora under construction. This is better-attested "scaffolding" than the theatre's, and I recommend moving the scaffolding here.
- **Bin.** Dedication date: K. Under construction in 53–55: R.

### C3. Stadium
A Hellenistic stadium is assumed on the site (attribution to Lysimachus is "most probable," not proven); it was rebuilt under Nero (AD 54–68) at the expense of C. Stertinius Orpex and family, by inscription. [web, secondary] So in the 50s a simpler stadium existed there. Keeping it off-map is fine. **R.**

### C4. Pollio monument and aqueduct (missing from the plan)
C. Sextilius Pollio built an aqueduct for the city in AD 4–14, and a monument to him stood at the west end of the state agora, set up by his stepson [memory; a related inscription is dated AD 4–14 by search]. The fountain later attached to it is AD 92/93 [memory]. So "Fountain of Pollio's later form" is correctly excluded, but the monument itself existed and can be added at the top of Curetes Street. **K** for existence, [memory] for details.

### C5. Other things that existed and are worth considering
- **Fishery toll-office at the harbor.** An inscription (IEph 20) dated AD 54–59 records the fishermen and fish-sellers of Ephesus building a toll-office with their own contributions, listed donor by donor. [web: Harland] It was built within a few years of Paul's stay, is a real working-people's association, and suits the harbor and fishmonger well. **K** (a year or two late for strictness; label "being built about now").
- **The governor's assizes.** The clerk's "the courts are open and there are proconsuls" is a real feature of Ephesus as an assize city. **K.**
- **Asiarchs** (Acts 19:31) are well attested in Ephesian inscriptions [memory]. **K.**
- **Coins.** Silver *cistophori* of Claudius show the temple of "Diana of Ephesus" with her statue [memory, unverified]. A moneychanger showing one would be a nice touch if confirmed.
- **Change of emperor.** Claudius died 13 October 54 and Nero succeeded, in the middle of the game's period. If the game is set at the riot (end of the stay, about AD 55), the emperor is the teenage Nero. Dialogue should not call Claudius the reigning emperor. **K.**

### C6. "Left out" list: all correctly excluded
All listed items are later than the 50s or are later traditions. [memory for most; Celsus Library and bouleuterion dates by web] Add these, which are also too late and easy to include by accident:

### C7. Everyday-life list: additions
Food and plants:
- No sugar (honey only), lemons (doubtful this early), bananas, rice as a staple, coffee, tea, chocolate, tobacco, vanilla, pumpkins or squash, green or kidney beans, peanuts, sunflowers, turkeys.
- No prickly-pear cactus, agave, eucalyptus or bougainvillea in the scenery. All are common around the ruins today and all arrived much later.
- Fine to show: olives, figs, grapes, pomegranates, wheat and barley bread, lentils, chickpeas, onions, garlic, cheese, fish, fish sauce, wine, pepper as an expensive import.

Objects and technology:
- Clay jars (amphorae) and sacks, not wooden barrels. Baskets, not crates.
- No wheelbarrows, horse collars, nailed horseshoes, spinning wheels (use hand spindles), windmills, forks at table, soap bars, paper (papyrus and parchment only), spectacles, clocks (sundials and water clocks only), street lamps, chimneys.
- Ships: square sail, steering oars at the stern, no stern rudder, no triangular sails as the main rig.
- Blown glass vessels are fine (new and spreading). Oil lamps are clay.
- Numbers on stalls and signs are Greek letters, never 1, 2, 3.
- Signs and graffiti in Greek; Latin only on official Roman dedications.

People:
- Roman men are clean-shaven in the 50s; beards on Romans are a 2nd-century fashion. Greek philosophers and many Jewish men are bearded.
- No trousers, buttons, pockets or zips. Pins and brooches fasten cloaks.
- Enslaved people cannot be told apart by clothing. No collars or chains on household staff.
- Synagogue: no Star of David, skullcaps or modern prayer shawls. A menorah is a fair symbol. Scriptures are Greek scrolls.
- No "Bible," no New Testament, no chapter and verse. The word "Christian" was new and rare; believers called themselves "the Way" (Acts 19:9, 23), "disciples," "brothers and sisters."
- No seven-day weekend rhythm for most of the city; market days and festivals instead.

Look of the city:
- Statues and temple details were painted, not bare white.
- Much less marble than the ruins suggest: more stucco, timber and tile.
- No lighthouse is attested. [unverified]

Money (the plan's line is right):
- Denarii and drachmas, plus local bronze small change. 50,000 drachmas is roughly 50,000 days' pay for a labourer (R).

## D. Section 4, characters

### D1. Priscilla and Aquila: consistent for the whole period
- Acts 18:2: left Rome under Claudius's order (about AD 49; date from a late source, so "about").
- Acts 18:18–19: Paul leaves them at Ephesus (about AD 52).
- Acts 18:26: they teach Apollos there before Paul returns. In the game this must be told as something already past.
- 1 Cor 16:19, written from Ephesus (1 Cor 16:8): "Aquila and Priscilla greet you … together with the assembly that is in their house."
- Romans 16:3–5, about AD 56–57: back in Rome. So they left Ephesus at some unknown point around or after the end of Paul's stay.
- Verdict: present throughout. **K** (as text). Their shop location: **I.** Paul calls her Prisca; Acts uses Priscilla. Either is fine.

### D3. Other character notes
- **Demetrius.** Acts calls his products "silver shrines" (*naoi*). None survive; small terracotta and marble shrines do. Fine to show small silver models, labelled R.
- **Synagogue elder.** Titles attested for Ephesus (later inscriptions) are "ruler of the synagogue" and "elders" [memory]. The Jewish community itself is well documented by Josephus (*Antiquities* 14.223–230, 262–264; 16.167–173). **K.**
- **Sons of Sceva.** Acts calls Sceva "a Jewish chief priest." No such high priest is known from other sources; say only what Acts says.
- **Paul.** Acts 20:18–21 labelled as spoken later at Miletus: correct.
- **Epilogue.** Acts 20:1 says only that Paul left for Macedonia, not that he sailed from the harbor. Harbor farewell: **I.**

Overall: nothing in the plaques contradicts Acts 18–20, and I found no anachronism in the "known" text. Eleven plaques need a change: five because a "known" sentence is firmer than the evidence, one for wording, and five because the "imagined" line does not match what the scene shows or leaves out a liberty.

## P1. Plaques that need a change

### P1.1 mazaeusGate: the model departs from a known feature, and "imagined" does not say so
- **Problem.** The real gate has three arched passages (it stands re-erected on site) [web, secondary: "triple gateway"; memory for the arches]. The scene draws four piers under a flat top. "Its painted colours and details are our guess" does not cover that: the arches are K, not a detail.
- **Fix (text).** Imagined: "The gate is real and stood at this corner of the market. The real one has three arched openings; ours is simplified, and its colours are our guess." Better still, model three arches (a code matter, not mine).
- **Also (clarity, optional).** Known, second sentence: "Its inscription names their patrons: Augustus and his wife Livia, and Agrippa and his wife Julia, the emperor's daughter." Teens will not know who Julia is. Agrippa had died in 12 BC, so the gate honours him after his death; no need to say so.
- **Checked and fine.** Date 4/3 BC (Augustus's titles in the Latin text) [web: Livius.org]. "Patrons" is the inscription's own word (Latin *patronis*; Greek text IEph 3006) [web]. South-east corner of the agora: K [web, secondary; memory]. "Paul very likely walked through it": properly hedged, R.

### P1.2 octagon: "about 80 years old" is too exact
- **Problem.** The building is dated by style only: "late 1st century BC" (Vienna museum description), 50–20 BC in older accounts, and Augustan (27 BC–AD 14) according to others [web, secondary]. The 2025 study's radiocarbon range for the skull is 205–36 BC [web]. So its age in the 50s could be anywhere from roughly 60 to 100 years. My own earlier note (A8) offered "about 80"; this corrects it.
- **Fix.** Replace the first sentence with: "This eight-sided tomb was built in the later 1st century BC, so it was already old in Paul's day."
- **Arsinoe and the skull: fine.** "People once guessed Cleopatra's sister Arsinoe, but a 2025 study showed the skull from the tomb belonged to a boy of about 11 to 14" matches Weber and others, *Scientific Reports* 15, 943 [web]. The tone is right: "Who was buried here is not known" comes first and nothing implies the question is settled. Do not add that the boy "was" the tomb's owner in any stronger sense; the study only says the skull is his.

### P1.3 memmius: date needs a hedge
- **Problem.** "It was built between 50 and 30 BC" is one dating (the site marker and the original publication). Other accounts put it under Augustus [web, conflicting secondary sources].
- **Fix.** "It was probably built between 50 and 30 BC at the top end of the street."
- **Fine.** Grandson of Sulla: named so in the monument's inscription [web: Laflı and Buora abstract]. "The Roman general Sulla" is accurate and at the right level (he was also dictator; Ephesians remembered him for punishing the city in 84 BC [memory]). "Top end of the street", on the north side of the later Domitian Square: K [web, secondary]. Honorary, not a tomb: K [memory].

### P1.4 tollOffice: function and location are inferred
- **Problem.** The Greek is "toll-office of the fishery" (*telonion tes ichthyikes*). What it was for is debated: collecting tolls on fishing or fish sales, or (one scholar) more of a club house and market hall. "A customs house for the fish trade" states one reading as fact. "At the harbor" rests on where the stone was found (harbor area). The donors may not all have been fishermen. [web: Harland, *Associations in the Greco-Roman World*, search summary]
- **Fix.** Replace the "known" text with: "An inscription from AD 54–59, found in the harbor area, records that the fishermen and fish-sellers of Ephesus built a 'fishery toll-office' with their own money, and it lists the givers one by one. It was probably where tolls on the fish trade were paid. So the building was new, or still going up, around Paul's time."
- **Bin.** Inscription, date, donors: K. Function: R. Site at the harbor: R.

### P1.5 neronianHall: "the dead emperor Claudius"
- **Problem.** The inscription calls him the deified Claudius (the word is partly restored) [web: search summary of IEph 3003]. "Dead" loses the point, which is that he had just been declared a god, and that is what dates the text.
- **Fix.** "Its inscription dedicates it to Artemis, to the emperor Claudius, who had just died and been declared a god, to the new emperor Nero, to his mother Agrippina, and to the people."
- **Fine.** East side of the agora, along the theatre street: K. AD 54–59 (after Claudius's deification in October 54, before Agrippina's death in March 59): K [web]. "We guess it was still a building site": R, honestly labelled.

### P1.6 magnesianGate: date needs a hedge
- **Problem.** "Dates from the 3rd century BC" is stated flat. The Austrian research project says only that a Hellenistic date for the first gate "seems probable" (it is set into the Hellenistic wall and has the plan of a Hellenistic courtyard gate with towers) and that "crucial points of its history are not clear" [web: FWF project P19987 summary]. The three-passage rebuilding is put under Vespasian (AD 69–79) by popular sources only [web, weak].
- **Fix.** "This gate in the south-east wall was probably first built in the 3rd century BC, along with the walls."
- **Imagined: fine.** "Kept it plain, because the triple-arched version is probably later" is honest and correctly hedged. The 350–400 m figure is still [memory].

### P1.7 curetes: the name is modern (same treatment as Marble Street)
- **Problem.** The plaque uses "Curetes Street" as if it were the ancient name. It is a modern name; in antiquity the street was most probably called the Embolos, a name found in inscriptions from the street and the agora [web: Turkish Archaeological News summary]. The marbleStreet plaque is careful about this, so this one should be too.
- **Fix.** Replace the first sentence with: "This sloping street, probably called the Embolos in ancient times, climbs from the Triodos to the upper square in the valley between the two hills. 'Curetes Street' is a modern name."

### P1.8 priscilla: "imagined" does not match the scene
- **Problem.** "With rooms behind and above" describes rooms the scene does not draw (a single open-fronted workshop, no upper storey).
- **Fix.** "Nobody knows where they lived or worked. We chose this shop beside the market and show only the workshop, not the home where the church met."
- **Known text: fine.** Acts 18:2–3, 18:19 and 1 Cor 16:19 are paraphrased accurately.

### P1.9 precinct: "the shrines you see"
- **Problem.** The scene has one altar block, not shrines.
- **Fix.** "What stood inside is uncertain, so the altar you see is our guess."
- **Known text: fine.** Cassius Dio 51.20.6: in 29 BC Augustus gave permission for precincts of Roma and of his father Caesar at Ephesus and Nicaea [web in the earlier review]. "Allowed Ephesus to build" is a fair paraphrase; "probably" and "Not all scholars agree" are the right hedges.

### P1.10 terraceHouse: the rain-water pool
- **Problem.** A central rain-water pool is the mark of an Italian atrium house. The Ephesian terrace houses are Greek-style houses with a paved, colonnaded courtyard [memory; not re-checked]. "The layout is simplified" does not cover an added feature.
- **Fix.** "We decorated ours more plainly, in a first-century style. The layout is simplified, and the courtyard pool is our own touch."
- **Known text: fine.** Built early in the 1st century AD (Terrace House 2, under Tiberius) and "mostly from the next two centuries" for the visible décor both stand [web in the earlier review]. Most of what is on show belongs to the last phase before the houses were destroyed in the later 3rd century [memory].

### P1.11 artemisAltar: one ambiguous clause (minor)
- **Problem.** "…made here, outside, as was usual at Greek temples" is true of sacrificing outdoors, but coming straight after "on its west side" it can be read as saying the west side was usual. It was not: most Greek temples and altars face east (see A7).
- **Fix.** "Offerings to Artemis were made here in the open air, not inside the temple. That was normal for Greek worship."
- **Scene.** The altar's foundations were found west of the temple in the 1960s by A. Bammer: K [web: Kunsthistorisches Museum, Vienna]. A horseshoe-shaped enclosure open to the west is, as I recall, Bammer's own reconstruction and has been disputed [memory; I could not confirm the plan or its open side]. "Size, shape and decoration are our guess" is therefore the right label.
